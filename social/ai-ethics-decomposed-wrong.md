## Post

The AI ethics debate sorts people into camps: informed or ignorant, ethical or complicit, engaged or pure.

Refuse the tools and people call it purity politics. The serious ones use them. Raise Facebook’s role in Myanmar, child labor, or other harms, and the moral high ground flips to refusal.

The arguments get buried under the sorting. Questions that must be answered together get split apart.

Reading Christopher Alexander helped me see the same error in design. People split aesthetics from function, then produce designs that sacrifice one for the other. We do the same with AI. We split the tool from its use, usefulness from ethics, personal choice from collective rules, technical knowledge from the right to decide, and local gains from costs dumped elsewhere.

Take a support bot. It cuts tickets by solving problems or by blocking access to a human. Whether it helps depends on the design goals, the metrics the company tracks, the handoff rules, the remaining staff, and who can override bad answers.

“AI” covers a coding assistant, a support bot, a layoff story, a data center, and a surveillance system. A useful coding tool becomes evidence for engagement. Harmful surveillance becomes evidence for refusal. Neither settles the support bot.

What you can do depends on what you control. Engineers inspect the model and workflow. Workers refuse or bargain. Regulators limit what companies build.

Before you call a system useful or ethical, ask: What work changes? Who owns the result? Who does the extra review? Who pays the costs? Who can stop or change it? What evidence shows the work got better?
