## Working brief

- **Reader:** People making, funding, selling, or living with organizational AI decisions.
- **Job:** Separate recurring organizational decision failures from the new instruments LLMs give them.
- **Claim:** LLMs did not create these failures. They make them easier to stage, measure, and defend.
- **Boundary:** LLMs do introduce new capabilities and failure modes; this is not an argument that nothing changed.

## LinkedIn post

“AI Mania Is Eviscerating Global Decision-Making” is the latest “the sky is falling” essay making the rounds. It has some interesting evidence. But calling the pattern “AI mania” stops the analysis too early.

The essay reports ordinary work repackaged as AI, employees trying to hit token-use quotas, buyers choosing a demo over a useful project, and executives who cannot safely correct their customers’ claims. Some people may believe the hype; others may be protecting a contract, promotion, or job. Both have a reason to repeat the claim.

The vendor–customer example is the strongest. A vendor who contradicts a customer’s public claim about AI productivity risks the contract. That customer has repeated the same claim to a board, executives, or customers. A correction makes somebody look bad, while staying quiet protects a contract, job, or status. That is enough to keep the claim going.

The same logic is in the quota and demo stories. If a company rewards visible AI use, people will produce visible AI use. If a buyer needs an AI story, a flashy demo can beat a useful project.

In a funding request, `AI` can make the project sound strategic. In a promotion case, it can make an employee sound current. In a sales pitch, it can tell a buyer that the vendor is on board. None of that shows that the work improved.

Long before LLMs, companies did this with “digital transformation,” agile, cloud, and cost cutting: announce a priority, count visible activity, and call it a strategy before anyone could show that the work got better.

The emperor has been naked for a long time. LLMs just give people more convincing things to point at: a demo, a usage count, a pile of generated work.

The “AI mania” analysis throws out the baby with the bathwater. Some AI projects will waste time and money. Others will help. You find out by starting with a real problem, spelling out how an LLM might change the work, checking whether the result improved, and stopping if it did not.
